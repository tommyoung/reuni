From research output to industry use.

My background in social and consumer psychology helps me make academic work easier to understand, easier to evaluate, and easier to move into a practical conversation with industry.

Tommy Prayoga, S.Psi., M.Psi. Downstreaming Section Head, Technology Transfer, RTT BINUS University
The working method

Psychology is not the headline. It is how I reduce friction.

The practical question is why a strong research result can still stall before it reaches a partner. Social and consumer psychology give me a way to look at motivation, trust, perceived effort, relevance, and the way unfamiliar ideas are judged.

The gap is usually not a lack of information.

Researchers and partners can understand the facts and still hesitate. The job of technology transfer is to make the route from knowledge to action clearer, with the right evidence, format, and next step for the audience in front of us.

Research becomes more useful when people can judge what it means for them.

That can mean reframing a technical result around a business problem, making a pilot easier to imagine, or packaging evidence so a decision maker can assess it without decoding academic language first.

Social psychology

I look for the human friction around action: confidence, ownership, social norms, peer visibility, trust, and whether the next step feels possible.

Consumer psychology

I translate research through the questions a partner is likely to ask: Is it useful, compatible, credible, worth testing, and easy to explain internally?

Communication

I shape the same evidence differently for leaders, technical teams, public audiences, and researchers, while keeping the underlying claim consistent.

Partner path

Make the next move obvious.

The role is to reduce friction between an industry problem and an academic capability. The process does not need to begin with a large research engagement. It can start with a clear problem and a practical test.

Problem briefing

Define the business issue, user need, technology challenge, community problem, or policy question that needs academic capability.

Research match

Identify the researcher, lab, intellectual property, expertise, or applied project that may be relevant to the problem.

Pilot shape

Turn the match into something that can be reviewed, demonstrated, tested, or scoped without overcommitting either side.

Decision material

Package the evidence in a format that helps a partner judge fit, credibility, constraints, and the logic for continuing.

Communication

Explain the outcome appropriately to leadership, technical teams, users, public audiences, or other stakeholders who need to understand it.

What the work can look like

Different formats for different decisions.

The same academic evidence should not look identical in every context. The form changes depending on who needs to understand it and what they need to do next.

Research translation

Turn dense academic material into a decision-ready brief.

Keep the evidence intact, but reorganize it around the problem, relevance, practical implication, and what a partner would need to examine next.

Pilot framing

Make a research idea easier to test.

Show the context, the proposed trial, the evidence to watch, and the decision that the trial is meant to support.

Industry should not need to decode academia before finding value.
A practical standard for technology transfer
Evidence packaging

One evidence base can become several useful formats.

A policy brief for leaders, a product summary for business teams, a demo story for industry audiences, or a video for public visibility can all come from the same underlying research.

Where a partnership can start

Start with one clear problem and a practical conversation.

A strong industry partnership begins when research is understandable, testable, and relevant to a real decision. Technology Transfer helps shape the route from the problem to the right academic capability, then into something both sides can evaluate.
